## What the study was about

Computer programs can compute how "similar" two videos are. For videos of natural scenes and materials, however, it was not known whether such numbers correspond to what people perceive. We therefore collected human choices in a triplet task: for each triplet we determine which candidate the majority of observers found more similar to the reference, and compare that with the candidate that various computational similarity measures would select. The result will be how often each measure agrees with the human majority — and also how much people agree among themselves, which sets a ceiling that any computational measure can reach at all.

You saw some triplets more than once (sometimes with sides swapped), and for some the answer was obvious. Both serve to check data quality: the former measures how stable one person's judgements are, the latter verifies that answers were given attentively. None of this evaluates you personally; the results are used only in aggregate.

We deliberately do not state here which specific computational measures are compared or what outcome we expect, so as not to influence participants who have yet to take part. After data collection closes, everything will be described in the published paper and in the preregistered analysis plan.
